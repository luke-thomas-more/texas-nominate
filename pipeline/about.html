How this works

TX-NOMINATE places every member of the Texas House and Senate since statehood on a map using nothing but their record votes. Members who vote alike end up close together, and members who usually vote against each other end up far apart. It's a Texas version of DW-NOMINATE, the method behind Voteview's maps of Congress.

Reading the map

  • Every legislature from 1846 to 2025 is on one map. A member keeps a single position for their whole career, and careers overlap, so each legislature is tied to the ones before and after it by the members they share. A score of +0.4 means the same place in 1925 as in 2025.
  • The place is fixed, but what it stands for isn't. Left to right is the main line along which Texas votes split, and up and down is the second. Who sits where changes as the issues and parties change. Below the map on each page is a short guide to what the axes meant in those years; the full list is below.
  • Scores run from −1 to +1 and only describe positions relative to other Texas legislators. A "moderate" here is someone between the blocs in Austin, not a moderate by any national standard.
  • Only contested votes count. A vote counts as contested when at least 2.5% of those voting were on the losing side. Most record votes are near-unanimous and say nothing about who disagrees with whom. They're still listed in the vote explorer.
  • On a vote's page, the dashed line is the cutting line. It's where the model draws the split for that vote: members on the shaded side are expected to vote yea, members on the other side nay. Members on the "wrong" side are the surprises.

What the axes mean, era by era

The labels come from looking at who sits at each end in each era, which roll calls split along each axis, and where the members' home counties are. They describe tendencies, not verdicts on any member.

One map for 180 years?

Texas politics changed completely between 1846 and today, so we tested whether one shared map really fits every era. For each era we fitted a separate map using only that era's votes, hid one vote in ten from both the shared map and the era's own map, and asked each to predict the hidden votes. With a good starting point, the shared map predicts the hidden votes about as well as the era-only maps, or better, in every era from the antebellum legislatures to today. So we use one map and accept that its axes mean different things in different eras, as Voteview does for Congress.

Where the votes come from

  • 1846 to the 1990s: the Legislative Reference Library's scanned journals. The Library has scanned every House and Senate Journal since statehood. We read the vote lists from the scans' text, and re-read pages with Tesseract OCR where that text is too garbled, up to three readings in all. When no one reading has every name, the readings are combined name by name. Old journals print a vote as running lists of surnames ("Yeas—Messrs. Allen, Baker of Travis …—52"), often split across columns and pages.
  • 1995 on (House) and 1999 on (Senate): the Legislature's own journal website, which publishes clean text. The 1995–97 Senate comes from the Library's copies.
  • Who's who comes from the Library's Texas Legislators: Past & Present: names, parties, districts, counties and exact service dates, plus its lists of Speakers and presidents pro tempore. Early journals rarely record party, so most members before Reconstruction show as "not recorded".
  • The journals name members by surname ("Bell, C.", "Taylor of Collin", "Mr. Speaker"). Each name is matched to one member using that session's roster, initials, first names ("Allen, John" next to "Allen, Joe"), counties and service dates, allowing for OCR misreadings. Where the Senate prints only the nays and absentees, the yeas are everyone else serving that day. Likewise, many journals list every member under some heading (yeas, nays, absent, excused, present and not voting), so when one list comes up a few names short and exactly that many serving members appear nowhere, they are its missing names.
  • Every roll call is checked name by name against the totals printed in the journal. A roll call is used only if every name is matched (setting aside OCR specks and stray headings or dates caught in a list) and the counts agree, or come within one vote (the journal's own count is sometimes off; at worst one vote is missing, and a name too garbled to read is left out as that missing vote). Where the printed total is plainly wrong (a stray digit giving more votes than the chamber has seats, or a total a few off from lists that name every member exactly once), the lists are trusted instead. A vote credited to someone who wasn't in the chamber that day, a garbled name read as a member who had left or not yet arrived, is taken out. A roll call on a page that two days' journal files share is counted once, on its own day. {{EXCLUDED}} roll calls that couldn't be verified are left out rather than guessed, which is most of the gaps you'll see in the older legislatures. A few journals are missing altogether: the House Journals for 1861–62 and 1864 survive only as newspaper reconstructions with almost no roll calls.
  • From 1973 to 1982 the House printed its votes as tally sheets, not lists of names: a grid of members with a mark in each one's yea, nay or present column (printed dots and clerks' pen checks through 1978, computer printouts after). We read the marks from the page images and line each sheet's rows up with that session's members. Each day's sheets are lined up with its record votes in order, and a sheet is used only if its yeas and nays add up to the journal's totals or fall one vote short of them, which about {{TALLY_SHARE}} do.

The method

DW-NOMINATE (Poole and Rosenthal) gives each legislator an ideal point and each roll call two outcomes, yea and nay, placed in the same space. A legislator tends to vote for whichever outcome is closer, with some randomness. The positions that best explain every vote at once are the map. Each member keeps one position for their whole career, which is DW-NOMINATE's "constant" model. We tried letting positions drift in a straight line, as Voteview does for Congress, but that let the whole map slowly rotate, so that "left–right" meant something different in one decade than another. Holding positions fixed keeps the map itself stable. The House and Senate share one scale, linked by the {{BOTH}} members who served in both chambers.

A map this big is easy to get stuck on a poor solution, so the fit starts from the era-by-era maps, joined into one frame through the members who served in neighbouring eras, and then refines everything together.

We wrote our own implementation from the published model, because Voteview's code isn't licensed for reuse. To check it, we ran it on U.S. House votes from the {{VV_CONG}} Congresses (2007–2022). In one dimension our scores correlate {{VV_R1}} with Voteview's published DW-NOMINATE scores ({{VV_RW}} or better within each party). Second dimensions are harder to compare. Voteview's comes from one fit of every Congress since 1789, anchored by the era of the Solid South, while a fit of 2007–2022 alone finds that era's own second divide, so the two correlate only {{VV_R2}}.

Against Shor and McCarty's state legislator scores (1993 on), the standard academic measure, our first dimension correlates {{SM_R}} across {{SM_N}} Texas legislators: {{SM_RD}} among Democrats and {{SM_RR}} among Republicans. Theirs combine roll calls with a national candidate survey, so they won't match exactly.

We also scaled each legislature's House and Senate on its own with W-NOMINATE, the single-session version of the model, using its authors' R package (Poole, Lewis, Lo and Carroll). Each of those fits picks its own orientation, so we turn it to line up with ours before comparing. Across {{WN_N}} sessions since 1961, the median correlation with our map is {{WN_R1_NEW}} on the first axis and {{WN_R2_NEW}} on the second. Before 1961 the agreement is weaker on the first axis, {{WN_R1_OLD}}, against {{WN_R2_OLD}} on the second, across {{WN_N_OLD}} sessions. We haven't yet traced why. Those older sessions are mostly smaller (a median of {{WN_MEMBERS_OLD}} members scaled), and our map gives each member one position for their whole career, while a single-session fit sees only that session's votes.

How much does the map depend on our judgment calls about which votes to trust? We refitted it three ways. Using only roll calls whose lists match the journal's printed totals exactly, or leaving out the Senate votes where we filled in the yeas as everyone else serving, the map barely moves: in every era it agrees with ours to {{SENS_R}} or better on both axes, turned by at most {{SENS_T}}°. Leaving out the 1973–82 House tally sheets matters more. They are the House's only record for those ten years, and they help tie the older map to the modern one; without them the eras before 1973 move by at most {{SENS_TALLY_T0}}°, but the eras since turn by up to {{SENS_TALLY_T}}° (agreement {{SENS_TALLY_R}} or better). So the tally sheets, which we read from page images, carry real weight in how today's map lines up with the past.

Caveats

  • Record votes aren't the whole story. Much of the Legislature's work happens in committee and in what never comes to a vote. Before the 1970s, many votes were taken by voice and never recorded name by name.
  • Older legislatures have fewer verified votes, because the scans are harder to read. A member with few contested votes has a less certain position.
  • The Speaker rarely votes, so a Speaker's position comes mostly from their votes before taking the gavel.
  • Members with fewer than 20 contested votes aren't placed on the map, though their votes still appear in the explorer.
  • "Liberal" and "conservative" are labels for the axis, not verdicts on any member. In some eras the axis tracks something else, such as who was in the majority and who opposed it; the era guide says where.
